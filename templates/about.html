About

Research tools forgot about evidence.

Most note apps are good at capturing text and bad at capturing why you believed it. Compendium is built the other way around.

Why it exists

Anyone who researches seriously runs into the same wall. You read widely, take notes, and form a view. Months later you need to defend that view — and you can no longer reconstruct which paper, report, or dataset convinced you. The conclusion survived; the justification didn't.

The usual tools encourage this. A document is a flat wall of prose. A folder is a pile of files with no argument attached. Neither knows the difference between something you claimed and something that proves it.

Compendium treats that relationship as the important part. A claim is a real object. Evidence attaches to it. The link between them is the thing being stored, not an afterthought you hope to remember.

The model

Everything is built on four nested levels, deliberately kept few enough to hold in your head:

  • Domains — the broad subject areas you work in.
  • Topics — the specific questions inside a domain.
  • Statements — individual claims, findings, or conclusions.
  • Evidence — the links, documents, images, video, tables, and notes supporting each statement.

That hierarchy is the whole system. There's no tagging scheme to invent and no methodology to adopt before you can write your first note.

Principles

Evidence over prose. A claim without support is visibly incomplete. The interface makes that obvious rather than hiding it.

Structure you can see. Claims and their evidence sit side by side. You should never have to scroll somewhere else to check your own reasoning.

Mixed sources are normal. Real research is a PDF, a spreadsheet, a video, and a half-remembered blog post. All of them are first-class here.

Durable by default. The value of a research tool shows up years later. Compendium is built so an old topic still explains itself.

Who it's for

Analysts building a case from mixed sources. Students keeping a literature review honest. Engineers recording why an architecture decision was made. Anyone who has had to answer "what makes you say that?" and wanted a better response than a vague recollection.
